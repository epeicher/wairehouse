In short

It is a churn problem, not a downloads problem. Weekly downloads have been flat for two months, but with 30 releases in four months nearly all of them are updates. New-install acquisition is close to zero, and the May launch cohort is draining faster than anything replaces it.

We are stuck in Generate. High output (two releases a week, a blog post every three days, a brand system) with no pipeline from release to audience to install, no measurement of what converts, and no customization where our differentiation actually lives.

Proposed targets: hold 2,000+ through October, 3,000 by end of December, 5,000 by end of Q1 2027. These are commitments to the levers below, not forecasts.

Bet 1

Stop the bleed

A retention loop we can measure: first-run activation, a deactivation feedback dialog, support hygiene, a compatibility push. Net flat by end of October.

Bet 2

Turn on Automattic's own distribution

WordPress.com, VIP, Jetpack, WooCommerce, Studio and Playground are channels we own and are not using. The one lever with the scale to move the number by thousands.

Bet 3

Reposition for what the market is buying

Keep the workspace as the visual hook. Lead the enterprise and press story with the governed AI workbench, which today is hidden from the listing.

Downloads are updates

  • The May cohort is the whole install base. The week of May 20 did 4,615 downloads, with 1,238 on May 21 alone. That was the hackathon and launch buzz. Nothing since has come close.
  • Every release generates about 2,000 update downloads over the following week. Weekly totals of 1,700 to 2,900 are an install base updating, plus a trickle of new installs.
  • The two spikes on the wp-rankings chart (Aug 27, Sep 3) are artifacts, not growth. They line up with the 1.1.4/1.1.5 and 1.1.6 releases. wp-rankings estimates installs partly from download velocity.
  • Net loss of 75 a week with gross adds under 60 a day means deactivations run somewhere between 300 and 500 a week. That is the number to attack first, and nothing in the product records why a site leaves.
  • Caveat. Playground launches from the openstation.me demo may count as wp.org downloads. If they do, organic acquisition is lower still. Measuring demo launches separately resolves this.

Why the cohort churns (hypotheses to test)

  1. Curiosity installs. "Automattic built a desktop for wp-admin in a hackathon" is a try-it story, not a keep-it story.
  2. Per-user opt-in hides the value. The plugin does nothing until a user clicks the admin-bar toggle. Site owners who installed for a team may never have seen it turn on anywhere.
  3. Compatibility friction. Windows over plugin admin pages break in ways the compat layer has not seen. At 0% support resolution, a break is a reason to leave and not come back.
  4. No re-engagement. Two releases a week ship real improvements, but the only channel to an installed site is a changelog written for engineers.
  5. The AI story is invisible. WPVibe pulled 6,000 installs in ten weeks on "AI manages your site". Our agents are screenshot 4 and absent from the tags.

The guide's five questions, answered for our stack

  1. Where are we using AI across content and marketing workflows? Ad hoc, per person, for drafting. No shared prompts, no guardrails, no measurement.
  2. Which workflows create the most friction? Release to audience. Two releases a week and no repeatable way to turn them into a story, a listing update, a social post and a re-engagement touch. Second: knowing whether anything worked.
  3. Where is out-of-the-box enough, and where does customization differentiate? Drafting, translation, captions and changelog rewriting are Generate work and fine with a voice file. The demo, onboarding and the enterprise narrative are Customize work nobody else can do for us.
  4. Do we have governance and ownership? No named owner for the North Star, no weekly review, no definition of "new install" versus "update".
  5. What manual work still exists? Every release note is rewritten by hand at least twice. Metrics are checked by visiting wp-rankings.

3. The model: installs = acquisition minus churn

Active installs is a stock. The flows are new installs, deactivations and deletions. Every play below is tagged with the flow it moves. Three rules:

  • Retention first. At the current rate, every 100 new installs a week only buys 25 net. Fixing churn multiplies every acquisition play.
  • Owned distribution before earned. Automattic's channels reach millions of WordPress sites. A single WordPress.com placement outweighs a year of blog posts.
  • One message, two registers. The workspace is the visual hook for creators and agencies. Governed AI is why enterprise teams and the press pay attention. Same product, same demo, different first sentence.

4. Positioning

Current: "wp-admin forgets. OpenStation doesn't." on the listing, and "One tab. Your whole WordPress site." on the site. Both true, both productivity claims, and neither says why a site should install it this quarter.

Proposed umbrella: The workspace where humans and AI run a WordPress site together. Built by Automattic, open source, nothing leaves your server.

  • Creator and agency register (listing, site, video): "Open posts, media and settings side by side. Ask the assistant to find anything. Give an agent a job and watch it work, on your desktop, under your rules." Lead visual: an editor, the Media Library and an agent's activity window open at once.
  • Enterprise and press register (VIP, WP Tavern, SEJ): "WPVibe puts your site inside someone else's AI client through a closed relay. OpenStation puts governed AI inside your admin: read-only Copilot, agents that are WordPress users with a capability ceiling, every tool an Ability you can audit, no API keys or metering. Open source, GPL, by Automattic."

Concrete changes

  1. Listing title and tags. Keep "OpenStation" first. Add the AI surface: ai-agents ai-assistant command-palette chatgpt claude. wp.org search weights title, tags, freshness, installs and support resolution. Today a search for "AI agent" cannot find us.

7. The marketing AI stack

The guide's core advice: audit Generate, connect a few things in Compose, customize only where it differentiates, name owners. Here is that stack for a team of two or three.

Generate: one voice, reusable

  • A voice file in the warehouse repo: the umbrella message, the two registers, banned words, three paragraphs from the readme that already sound right. Every AI-assisted draft starts from it.
  • A prompt library for the recurring jobs: PR list to human changelog; changelog to What's New; release to blog draft; blog to social snippets; English to Spanish, French and Japanese; screenshot captions; support first reply.
  • Approval stays human. Roberto approves anything public. The readme voice is an asset and brand drift is real.
  • Measure downstream. A post counts if it produced demo launches or installs, not because it shipped.

Compose: three integrations, owned

  1. Release to content. A GitHub Action on tag collects merged PR titles, drafts the human changelog and What's New lines with the voice file, opens a draft post on openstation.blog through the REST API, and opens a Linear task for review. Nothing publishes automatically.
  2. Metrics to review. A daily script pulls the wp.org info and download APIs, GitHub stars, the wp-rankings estimate, site and demo counters, and the deactivation table into one dashboard. A weekly Slack post with the North Star and leading indicators.
  3. Support to triage. A daily check of the wp.org forum opens a Linear issue per new thread with a reply draft attached. First reply within 48 hours becomes mechanical.

Do not connect anything else this quarter. The guide's "connected but fragile" pattern is real at any team size.

Customize: where only we can win

  • The Playground demo as a guided tour. A real-looking site, three windows pre-arranged, a Space per job, an agent with one visible completed run, a five-step overlay. Instrument launches and step completion. It is the product's best salesperson and today it is a blank sandbox.
  • The retention loop. Deactivation reasons and first-run activation feed a monthly "why sites leave" review that picks one fix per month.
  • The Automattic go-to-market. Nobody else can put OpenStation on WordPress.com, in Studio, or in a VIP guide. This is customization in the guide's exact sense.

10. What not to do

  • Do not chase the downloads chart. More releases lift downloads without lifting installs, and we already ship twice a week. Cadence is fine; narrative is missing.
  • Do not add telemetry without opt-in. wp.org rules and our "nothing leaves your server" message both forbid it. The deactivation dialog and first-run counter are opt-in and anonymous.
  • Do not build a relay or a SaaS. The self-hosted, no-key story is the differentiator against WPVibe.
  • Do not rewrite the readme's first paragraph. It is the best-written thing in our marketing. Add to it.
  • Do not connect more than three systems this quarter. The guide's "connected but fragile" pattern is one weekend of enthusiasm away.
